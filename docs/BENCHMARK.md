# Benchmark Method

The research question is: how does containerized RDF monitoring scale when monitored population and actual ABox grow while ontology, SPARQL catalog, resources, hardware, RDFS semantics, and concurrency remain fixed?

Independent variables are users (`1, 50, 100, 500, 1000, 2000, 4000, 5000, 6000, 7000, 10000, 12000, 14000, 16000, 18000, 20000`) and layout (`distributed`, `replicated`). This studies ABox growth rather than TBox complexity. The 4000--7000 extension follows a fully successful 2000-user physical campaign. The 10000--20000 range uses 2000-user increments after a fully successful 7000-user campaign and intentionally locates the memory, CPU, and deadline frontier with greater resolution. Controlled values are concurrency one, one container per host, one CPU, 1536 MiB RAM, the fixed 113-query catalog, 18 complete catalog rounds, one warm-up, three measured repetitions, and seed 2026. One round means one serial execution of the complete logical catalog; fan-out may produce more physical executions in `distributed`.

The canonical scenario dataset is generated once and reused byte-for-byte. Dataset generation time is recorded and excluded from preparation. A reference partition is computed only to establish expected fragment and closure digests. Warm-up is stored separately and excluded from statistics. Every warm-up and measured repetition independently resets, partitions, serializes, transfers, parses, loads, reasons, validates, executes the catalog, samples resources, and persists evidence from fresh state.

Preparation is `T_partition + T_serialize + T_transfer + T_remote_load`. `T_reasoning` is measured and reported separately. Per-node evidence includes serialized/payload bytes, graph and closure digests, and triples before, after, and inferred by RDFS. `T_transfer` is the coordinator-observed request duration after subtracting worker-reported parsing/store and reasoning time; it is not a packet-level network measurement.

The operational target is 120 s and hard deadline 600 s; query deadline is 120 s. The independent preparation-body ceiling is 256 MiB and is validated against every serialized fragment before network transfer. Its exact value is part of worker health evidence. States distinguish `completed_within_target`, `completed_over_target`, `hard_timeout`, `query_timeout`, `failed`, `oom_killed`, `payload_limit_exceeded`, `incompatible_worker`, and `semantic_mismatch`. A timeout is categorical and is never converted into a duration equal to its deadline. An OOM-killed worker is recorded first and then restarted from an empty graph so later repetitions remain independent and executable. `--diagnostic-unlimited` removes only the preparation-request deadline, retains the SPARQL safety deadline and body ceiling, and marks the whole run non-comparable.

Docker/cgroup data are sampled with a one-second target interval. Raw timestamps retain the achieved interval. Summaries report CPU mean, p95, peak and counter delta, container-memory mean, p95 and peak, network counter deltas when Docker exposes them, and OOM state. Three repetitions support descriptive medians and observed ranges; they do not justify strong inferential claims.
